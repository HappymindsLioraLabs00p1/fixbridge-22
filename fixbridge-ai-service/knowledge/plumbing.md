# Plumbing — approved homeowner procedures

Source: FixBridge approved procedures, revision 1

## Leak at a compression fitting under a sink

A slow drip where two pipes join under a basin is usually a compression fitting that has worked
loose, or a worn washer inside it. This is one of the few plumbing faults a homeowner can safely
attempt, because the water can be isolated at the fixture and the fitting tightens by hand.

Isolate the water first. Under most sinks there are two small isolation valves, one per supply
pipe. Turn each clockwise until it stops, then open the tap above to confirm the flow has stopped
and to release pressure still in the pipe. If a valve will not turn, or there are no isolation
valves, stop — shutting off the whole property is a plumber's job.

Dry the area completely before inspecting. Water travels along pipes and collects at the lowest
point, so a wet patch is often nowhere near the actual leak. With everything dry, the first place
water reappears is the source.

Tighten a plastic compression nut by hand only, clockwise, until it is firm. These fittings seal
against a rubber or nylon washer; a wrench cracks the plastic and turns a drip into a flood. If the
nut is already hand-tight and still leaking, the washer has failed and the fitting needs opening —
that is where a homeowner should stop.

Restore the water slowly. Open the isolation valve about a quarter turn, wait, then open it fully.
Run the tap for thirty seconds while watching the joint. A seal that has failed will seep gently
under low pressure rather than spraying under full pressure.

Stop and call a plumber if: water appears from the pipe body rather than a joint, the pipe is
copper and soldered, the leak is behind a wall or under a floor, or the water is hot and you cannot
isolate it.

## Blocked sink drain

A sink that drains slowly but does drain is usually blocked in the trap — the U-bend directly
beneath the plughole. A sink that does not drain at all, or where other fixtures back up at the
same time, indicates a blockage further along the system and is not a homeowner job.

Try mechanical clearing before any chemical. A plunger, sealed over the plughole with the overflow
covered, will shift most trap blockages. Chemical drain cleaners are caustic, damage older pipes,
and make the job hazardous for whoever opens the trap afterwards.

To clear the trap, place a bucket underneath, unscrew both compression nuts by hand, and lower the
trap out. Expect standing water. Clear it, rinse it, and refit hand-tight.

Stop if: several fixtures are backing up together, there is sewage odour, or water is rising rather
than draining. Those indicate a blockage in the soil stack or drain, which needs professional
equipment.

## Dripping tap

A tap that drips when fully closed has a worn washer or ceramic cartridge. Replacing one is within
reach for a homeowner, but only after the supply is isolated and only on a tap that can be
dismantled without force.

Isolate the supply and open the tap to drain it. Note which way the handle came off and photograph
each stage before disassembly — reassembly order matters more than the repair itself.

Do not attempt this on a mixer tap fed directly from a hot cylinder without isolation, or where the
tap body turns in the basin when you apply force. A turning tap body means the fixing nut beneath
has failed, which is a different and larger job.
